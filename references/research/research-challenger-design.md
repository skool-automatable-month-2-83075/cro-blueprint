That dossier settled the statistics: leads not clicks, leads per arm ≈ 16/lift², structural variants only, two full weeks, no peeking, 13% winner's-curse haircut, pool across a portfolio. This one answers a different question: given that the test is affordable, **how is the second page derived**, how many candidates should exist, and where does the hypothesis come from. It does not re-argue sample size except where an extra arm changes it.

## Findings

### 1. One challenger or several?

- **The exact cost of an extra arm, computed.** With k test arms against one control, uncorrected family-wise alpha is 1-(1-0.05)^k: 9.8% at k=2, 14.3% at k=3, 22.6% at k=5 (source 1, formula; verified arithmetically). Bonferroni sets per-comparison alpha to 0.05/k; Šidák to 1-(0.95)^(1/k), which differs from Bonferroni by under 2% at these k and is not worth the extra explanation. Grade (a) for the formula, (b) for the arithmetic below.
- **Leads per arm at 30% MDE, 80% power, two-sided, Bonferroni-corrected** (recomputing the repo's own 16/lift² with the corrected z): 174 for a 2-arm test; 211 per arm at 3 arms (+21%); 233 at 4 arms (+33%); 248 at 5 arms (+42%). Grade (b), derived from source 6's n = 16σ²/δ².
- **The total-leads figure is the one that decides it, and it is much worse than the per-arm figure**, because the extra arm needs its own sample *and* raises everyone's bar. Total leads needed across all arms at 30% MDE: 349 for two arms, 634 for three (1.82x), 931 for four (2.67x), 1,557 for six (4.46x). Grade (b).
- **In months, at this repo's traffic.** At 30 leads/month across both arms: 11.6 months for A/B, 21.1 for A/B/n with two challengers, 31.0 with three. At 100 leads/month: 3.5 / 6.3 / 9.3. At 300 leads/month: 1.2 / 2.1 / 3.1 months. Grade (b). **A third arm is defensible only above roughly 300 leads/month**, where it costs about one extra month; at 30-100 leads/month it converts a viable test into an abandoned one.
- **Sample pollution makes the real cost worse than the alpha maths.** Ton Wesseling (source 1): with one variation a returning visitor who cleared cookies or switched device lands in the same arm 50% of the time; with three variations, 25%. "The more variations, the bigger the pollution... with a long lasting experiment the samples will be so polluted that they are almost identical." Pollution biases every arm toward the grand mean, i.e. toward calling everything flat. This penalty compounds with duration, which is exactly the regime a multi-arm low-traffic test lives in. Grade (c) practitioner, but mechanistically sound and directly relevant.
- **Wesseling's conclusion is the one that matches this repo's traffic**: "most websites don't have enough users and conversions to run multiple variations experiments - so please always tell people to start with one... it makes more sense to have an experiment with just one bold variation" and "better to run 10 A/B experiments on several locations of your site than just one big experiment on one location - you will gather more behavioral insights." Grade (c).
- **The strongest pro-multi-arm case comes with its own traffic floor, and this repo is two orders of magnitude below it.** Andrew Anderson (sources 1, 2) argues all test ideas are fungible, that you should "maximise the beta (range of feasible options), not the delta", that "anything with less than four variants is a no go", and that he will "prioritise tests with 10 options over tests with five even if I think the change for the five is more likely to be impactful". He then states the precondition explicitly: "I only suggest following this course of optimization for sites that have at least 500 conversions a month with a 1000 per month minimum more reasonable", and that his lowest-traffic sites, at ~10 conversion events per day (~300/month), get only 4-5 alternatives, while "sites that would never be manageable below that mark we do not do testing on and look for other ways to optimize." Grade (c) opinion, but from a stated 300-organisation practice, and the threshold is the usable part.
- **Practitioner consensus caps out around five even at high traffic.** Julia Engelmann (konversionsKRAFT, source 1): "I would never test more than five variations (including control) at the same time", and "if it's a low-traffic website, I would generally recommend to test with less variations but high contrast." Grade (c).
- **Platforms all correct automatically across variant count, which removes the maths excuse but not the power cost.** Optimizely uses a tiered Benjamini-Hochberg across both metrics and variations, targeting ~10% FDR, exempting the primary metric (source 13). Eppo uses preferential Bonferroni with the primary metric receiving γα/k where k is the number of treatment variants (source 14). Statsig auto-applies Bonferroni or BH on metric-and-variant count and advises limiting both (source 15). Grade (a) for the procedures, (b) for the vendor defaults. Note that all three make power a function of k, so the platform silently charges the sample-size penalty computed above whether or not you notice it.
- **FDR instead of FWER is the correct relaxation if a third arm is ever justified**, since it bounds the expected proportion of false positives among declared winners rather than the probability of any false positive, and Holm-Bonferroni is uniformly more powerful than plain Bonferroni at no cost (source 17, grade (a)). Neither rescues a 3-arm test at 30 leads/month.
- **Does generating several candidates and picking ONE beat designing one?** This is the question with the least direct evidence, and what exists points two ways.
  - For: ResearchXL's documented step is that a cross-functional team "hypothesise as many solutions to the problems as possible", with variety explicitly valued because "sometimes the idea that nobody believes in wins" (source 24, grade (c)). CXL's low-traffic alternatives article documents a concrete protocol for *picking* between candidates without spending traffic: split 20-50 recruited users into two groups, show each group only one page first to avoid anchoring, then both groups a marked and unmarked side-by-side, and collect **written** answers because spoken feedback is "often superficial" (source 4, grade (c), n>1,000 recordings of author experience).
  - Against, and this is the harder evidence: human and model ability to pick the winner among candidates is near-worthless. Microsoft's own distribution is one third win, one third flat, one third lose (source 6's Table 2 lineage), and Kohavi's summary of the literature is that people are systematically bad at predicting which idea will work. The LLM result is sharper: on 330 real A/B tests a multimodal judge scoring screenshots reached Cohen's κ = 0.141 [0.034, 0.248] unconditional, and on the statistically significant subset the evidence was inconclusive at κ = 0.108 [-0.049, 0.264] (source 8, grade (a), pre-registered, n=330). A 2.8x more expensive frontier model was statistically equivalent to the cheap one. Grade (a).
  - **The one thing that did work in source 8 is abstention, not prediction.** Gating on internal panel agreement at vote-margin ≥ 0.6, the judge called 49% of tests and on significant-only labels reached κ = 0.311 [0.025, 0.555] - the only operating point that survived the authors' own label audit, and flagged exploratory because a stricter pre-registered unanimity check on 33 calls returned κ = -0.100. Read as: a selection procedure among candidates has *some* value precisely when several independent passes agree, and none when they do not. Grade (a) but explicitly exploratory.
  - **Source 8 also contains a warning that applies directly to any pattern library used to pick a challenger**: 44% of the ground-truth labels in a leading CRO agency's curated case library come from non-significant tests, and the judge agreed *more* with those unreliable labels than with the reliable ones - "the signature of a shared prior between label curator and model, not of prediction." Grade (a). Any "best practice" the repo inherits from a swipe file is contaminated the same way.
- **Variant quality vs quantity, the only outcome data either way.** Win rate varies far more by what kind of change was made than by how many were tried. Across 2,288 audited tests, copy/messaging won 60.0% raw and social proof 56.7%, while layout won 43.3% and forms 43.4%; the source's own gloss is that structural changes like forms "take more attempts to crack" (source 9, grade (b), n=2,288). Across 6,700 large e-commerce experiments, behavioural-psychology changes outperformed cosmetic UI modifications significantly (source 23 citing Browne & Jones 2017, grade (b)). Optimizely's read on 127,000 experiments is "You don't need to run 200 tests a year. You need to run 10 good ones. Run fewer. Run braver." (source 11, grade (a) dataset / (c) interpretation).

### 2. How practitioners actually derive the challenger

- **ResearchXL (Peep Laja / CXL)** is a research pipeline, not a variant generator. Six components: technical analysis (device, browser, speed), heuristic analysis (up to 6 people rating friction / distraction / motivation / relevance, producing "areas of interest"), digital analytics (tracking completeness, funnel leaks), mouse tracking (scroll, click, session replay), qualitative research (surveys, polls, interviews, chat logs), user testing (5-10 people is enough). Output is a master list of *problems*; the variant is whatever a cross-functional team hypothesises against a problem (source 24, grade (c)). **No outcome data is published for the framework itself** - the model is a method for finding problems, and its evidentiary claim is about input quality, not measured win rate.
- **LIFT (Chris Goward / WiderFunnel, now Conversion.com)** is a checklist for *reading* a page, and it maps almost exactly onto what invert.py already measures. Value proposition is the core; Relevance, Clarity, Urgency are drivers; Anxiety and Distraction are inhibitors (source 25, grade (c)). Conversion.com's own framework page publishes **no** win rates or lift figures. The circulating numbers - 23.1% average lift for e-commerce, 49.0% for lead gen, client range 10% to 277% - are WiderFunnel self-reported aggregates carried by third parties (source 26, grade (c), no sample size, no methodology, selection bias unaddressed). **Do not treat 49% as an expected lift for a lead-gen test**; it contradicts every measured dataset in this dossier (median winner +1.88%, source 10; mean 3.77%, source 23).
- **PXL (CXL)** is prioritisation, and it is the most directly machine-applicable framework found. Binary 0/1 scoring, weighted 0/2 on the three variables CXL considers decisive. The impact questions: is the change above the fold; **is the change noticeable in under 5 seconds** (operationalised as: show people control then variation for 5 seconds, can they tell the difference - if not it will have less impact); does it add or remove anything (bigger changes, e.g. removing distractions or adding key information, tend to have more impact); does it run on a high-traffic page. The four evidence questions, each scored 0/1: is it addressing an issue found via user testing; via qualitative feedback (surveys, polls, interviews); supported by mouse-tracking heatmaps or eye tracking; found via digital analytics. Ease is bracketed by estimated dev time (source 5, grade (c)). The stated design goal is inter-rater agreement, and the 5-second test plus the add/remove test are exactly the "is this challenger different enough" gate this repo needs.
- **Booking.com** is a volume-and-infrastructure practice, not a variant-design method, and the published paper contains no guidance on how to design the B page. What it does document: every change - full redesigns, infrastructure changes, bug fixes - is wrapped in an experiment; a central repository records **failures as well as successes** so knowledge is shared; loose coupling between experiment and business logic; end-to-end ownership by whoever proposes the test (source 28, grade (b), 10+ years of practice, no counts given). Volume: >1,000 concurrent tests, >25,000/year, and a ~10% success assumption (source 27, grade (b), snippet only). The transferable part at 30 leads/month is the failure repository, not the volume.
- **Microsoft / Kohavi** publish the outcome distribution and the statistical discipline, not a variant-design recipe. The distribution: roughly a third of ideas positive-and-significant, a third flat, a third negative-and-significant, with Microsoft's 33% success rate paid for by heavy upfront scoping before an idea becomes an experiment (source 6 and its cited lineage, grade (a)). The design implication is about power, not creativity - see finding 3.
- **Optimizely and VWO documentation converge on the same three instructions for low traffic**, which is notable because they are commercial competitors: avoid multivariate, stay on a two-arm A/B until traffic supports more, and make the change big ("testing something big may have a more noticeable effect" - Optimizely, source 12; "apple or orange rather than red apple vs green apple" - VWO, source 16). Both add: measure something closer to the page (micro-conversions) and widen targeting to pool traffic. Grade (c) vendor guidance, but unanimous and consistent with the maths.
- **Which of these is backed by outcome data rather than opinion?** Honest ranking: PXL's *criteria* have no outcome data but high inter-rater reliability by design; ResearchXL and LIFT have none published; Kohavi/Microsoft and the 127,000/2,288/90-brand datasets have outcome data but describe *distributions*, not derivation methods. **No named challenger-design methodology in this dossier is backed by a controlled comparison against another design method.** That absence is itself the finding: methodology choice here is a bet on plausibility, so prefer the one that is cheapest to follow and hardest to fudge.

### 3. Radical redesign vs iterative change

- **The power argument is the whole argument at low traffic, and it is arithmetic, not taste.** n per arm = 16σ²/δ² (source 6, grade (a)). Detecting +10% needs about 1,500 leads a side; +30% about 180; +50% about 70. VWO's worked example: 500 visits/day at 2% baseline takes 1,254 days to detect a 5% lift and 13 days to detect a 50% lift (source 47 of the prior dossier, re-confirmed by source 16's framing). Radical change is not preferred because it is better design; it is preferred because it is the only effect size a 30-300 lead/month page can resolve.
- **Kohavi's position is not "make big changes" - it is "do not run underpowered experiments", and this cuts both ways.** Source 6's recommendation is that platforms should actively "discourage experimenters from starting underpowered experiments", because "with high probability, nothing statistically significant will be found, and in the unlikely case ... a statistically significant result is obtained, it is likely to be a false positive with an overestimated effect size." Grade (a). **This directly contradicts this repo's current rule "never refuse a test for low traffic: print the months and start."** The reconciliation the repo is implicitly betting on is that a large enough structural change raises the true effect size until the test is no longer underpowered - which is legitimate, but it makes "the change must be big" a *precondition for running at all*, not a stylistic preference.
- **The false-positive cost of low power, quantified.** Source 6's Table 3 gives False Positive Risk among stat-sig results, by organisational success rate and power. At 80% power: Microsoft (33% success) 5.9%, Kaushik (20%) 11.1%, Bing (15%) 15.0%, Booking/Google Ads/Netflix (10%) 22.0%, Airbnb Search (8%) 26.4%. At 50% power those become 9.1 / 16.7 / 22.1 / 31.0 / 36.5%. **At 20% power: 20.0 / 33.3 / 41.5 / 52.9 / 59.0%** - so at a 10% base success rate and 20% power, more than half of your significant winners are false. Grade (a). A local-service page is nearer the 10-15% success band than Microsoft's 33%, so the repo's 80% power default is doing more work than it looks.
- **The exaggeration problem is separate from and worse than the false-positive problem.** Gelman & Carlin: below 50% power the exaggeration ratio - expected absolute estimate divided by true effect - becomes "so high as to be meaningless"; Button et al. find effects inflated 25-50% in low-power regimes (source 6, grade (a)). This is the formal basis for the repo's existing 13% winner's-curse haircut, and it implies the haircut should scale with realised power, not be a constant.
- **The canonical cautionary example of a big swing is a Microsoft one and it is about segments, not size.** An MSN major redesign "looked fine conceptually" but the test revealed a sizeable segment with terrible metrics, traced to Samsung TVs defaulting to the MSN homepage on a browser never included in QA (Kohavi, source recounted via growthbook interview, grade (c) secondhand). The lesson that transfers: a radical challenger must be checked on the segments that were never designed for. For a local-service page the analogue is mobile, and the repo's new-vs-returning check already covers half of this.
- **NN/g's position is the strongest published case against radical redesign, and it is qualified in a way that favours this repo.** "Never make radical changes when minimal adjustments will suffice"; incremental is preferred because it is less disruptive, cheaper, lower risk of breaking something critical, and "users like designs that are safe and familiar". But the five conditions NN/g names as justifying a rebuild include "diminishing returns reached after years of gradual fixes", "architectural dysfunction after years of patchwork", and "extremely high exit and bounce rates persisting despite incremental efforts" (source 20, grade (c)). A never-tested local-service landing page satisfies none of NN/g's *incremental* preconditions either - there is no history of gradual fixes to be at diminishing returns from. NN/g's real warning is about motive: redesign driven by "I'm bored with it" while "customers likely aren't".
- **Does "change everything" produce bigger measurable effects?** The evidence says structural change wins *less often* but is where the unresolved questions live. Across 2,288 tests, layout 43.3% and forms 43.4% raw win rate versus copy 60.0% and social proof 56.7% (source 9, grade (b)). Across 6,700 experiments, behavioural changes beat cosmetic ones (source 23, grade (b)). These are not contradictory: copy tests win more often at small effect sizes; structural tests win less often but are the only ones with effects large enough for a 30-lead/month page to see. **At this traffic the repo cannot buy copy tests' higher win rate, because it cannot resolve their effect size.**
- **The "we don't know which change caused it" problem, and whether it matters here.** It is real and the sources are blunt about it: a low-traffic A/B "can't explain why one variation won, even if you test on high-traffic pages and reach 95%+ statistical significance" (source 4, grade (c)); and CXL's low-traffic advice is to implement all hypotheses at once anyway, explicitly accepting the loss of attribution (source 3, grade (c)). How teams handle it at scale is by decomposition - Booking wraps *every* change in its own experiment (source 28), which is only possible at 25,000 tests/year. **At 30-300 leads/month, attribution is not affordable and should not be pretended at.** The correct posture is that the unit of learning is the page shape, not the element; the axis-level "why" is recovered later by a sequential programme (finding 6), not by decomposing a single test.
- **One consequence the repo should absorb: a whole-page win is a claim about a shape, and the log should say so.** Recording "form-in-hero beat form-at-70% on a 550-word page" is a defensible claim; recording "moving the form up lifts leads 30%" is not, because six other axes moved with it.

### 4. Where the hypothesis should come from

- **No study found ranks research inputs by subsequent test win rate.** This was searched directly and repeatedly. What exists is one near-miss and a lot of assertion.
- **The near-miss, and it is the best available evidence on this question.** DRIP's dataset of 90+ European e-commerce brands (thousands of experiments, 2020-2026) reports a 36.3% stat-sig win rate against the VWO/CXL industry benchmark of roughly 25-30%, and attributes the gap to a single process difference: "DRIP pre-qualifies every hypothesis with quantitative analytics, session recordings, and heatmap data before building a test." The article is explicit that **no separate win rate is published for data-backed vs non-data-backed tests within their own dataset** (source 10, grade (b), and the causal claim is grade (c) because the comparison is against an external benchmark with different mix, geography and page types). Treat the direction as plausible, the magnitude as unestablished.
- **The 2,288-test audit makes the same claim with the same gap**: tests backed by user research or an analytics finding win more often than those from stakeholder opinion, "but no quantified comparison is provided" (source 9, grade (b) dataset / (c) claim).
- **PXL's answer is to weight the four inputs equally, which is a confession that nobody knows the ranking.** User testing, qualitative feedback (surveys/polls/interviews), mouse-tracking heatmaps or eye tracking, and digital analytics each score 0 or 1 (source 5, grade (c)). CXL's stated reason is not that these are the most predictive but that "ideas that come from opinions score lower" - the framework is built to exclude opinion, not to rank evidence.
- **The one input with a measured predictive claim is prior test results on the same pattern.** GoodUI: 51 experiments predicted positive from pattern history, 36 came back positive - 71% against a 50% chance baseline (source 21, grade (b), n=51, self-published, no correction for the patterns' own selection). Their library is 141 patterns from 640 tests with per-pattern replication from 2 to 14 (source 22). This is the strongest "where hypotheses come from" evidence in the dossier and it points somewhere the repo currently does not look: a log of what already won on similar pages.
- **But that same evidence class is compromised.** 44% of ground-truth labels in a leading CRO agency's curated case library come from non-significant tests, and an LLM judge agreed more with the unreliable labels than the reliable ones (source 8, grade (a)). Grade (b) pattern libraries must therefore be filtered to significant tests before being used as priors, or they encode a shared prior rather than evidence.
- **Ranking implied by evidence strength, not by measured win rate** (stated as such because no study supports a measured ranking):
  - Strongest, because it is the only input with a replication statistic behind it: **your own prior significant test results on a comparable page**, plus externally replicated patterns filtered to significant tests only (grade (b), sources 21, 22, 8).
  - Strong, because it is cheap, traffic-independent and named by every framework: **session replays of non-converting sessions** and **form analytics**. Source 3's worked example is exactly this repo's use case - a 4-step form with a large first-step drop-off, resolved by watching replays and finding the field where people stopped ("personal references - most people had none"), fixed by deletion (grade (c), single case, but the mechanism is direct observation of the failure).
  - Strong and specific to local service, though evidenced only by assertion: **sales-call objections**. Framed as "every sales objection is a recorded customer interview that nobody scheduled and nobody had to recruit for" (grade (c)). For a plumber or roofer doing 30 leads/month this is the highest-volume qualitative channel that exists - the phone answers more prospects than any survey will.
  - Moderate: **user testing at 5-10 people** (ResearchXL's own stated sufficiency, source 24, resting on the Nielsen 5-users-finds-80%-of-usability-issues claim, grade (c)) and **exit / post-purchase surveys** (one reported 33% conversion gain from a single language change sourced this way, grade (c), single uncontrolled case).
  - Weak at this traffic: **heatmaps and scroll maps**, because "you need statistically valid sample sizes to draw any conclusions" and a low-traffic site does not have them - CXL's own recommendation at low traffic is algorithmic attention prediction instead of real-user heatmaps (source 3, grade (c)). This is a direct demotion of an input PXL and DRIP both weight highly.
  - Weakest: **competitor teardowns and persona panels.** No outcome evidence found in any direction. Source 23's consensus warning applies: avoid "commoditizing experimentation by blindly copying patterns instead of conducting situation-specific research", and Longden's objection - "one website is radically and utterly different from another website... you just cannot control for it" - bites hardest here (grade (c)).
- **Heuristic analysis is the documented starting point when no data exists, and this is the honest description of a first test on a never-tested page.** CXL's low-traffic sequence begins with heuristic analysis across clarity, friction, distraction, urgency, traffic-source scent, and buying phase - and says plainly that it is "expert based", "not guaranteed to be optimal", and that "you can be dead wrong" (source 3, grade (c)). ResearchXL formalises the same thing with up to 6 raters (source 24). **A first challenger derived from page structure alone is heuristic analysis, which is a named and accepted method, not a failure of rigour - but it is the weakest tier and should be labelled as such in the test log.**

### 5. Multivariate testing

- **Every platform consulted says do not, and they are competitors.** Optimizely: "Avoid multivariate testing. Testing multiple variations requires more traffic, and results take longer to reach statistical significance" - stick with A/B "until you have sufficient traffic to direct to multiple variations" (source 12). VWO/Wingify: test only one or two variations, avoid MVT (source 16). AB Tasty: MVT is fully factorial, so traffic gets split into quarters, sixths, eighths or smaller, and in practice this means "longer tests and an inability to reach statistical significance, especially for pages deep within your site with low traffic, such as order tunnels or landing pages". Grade (c) unanimous vendor guidance.
- **The maths, for this repo's page.** A full factorial over the seven axes invert.py already measures, each binary, is 2^7 = 128 cells. At 30% MDE and Bonferroni across 127 comparisons against the reference cell, alpha per comparison is 0.05/127 ≈ 0.0004, which at 80% power needs roughly 380 leads per cell, or about 49,000 leads total. At 300 leads/month that is 162 years. Even the smallest sane MVT - three axes, 8 cells, alpha 0.05/7 - needs about 235 leads per cell, 1,880 total, 6.3 months at 300 leads/month and 5.2 years at 30. Grade (b), derived from source 6's formula plus source 1's correction formula.
- **MVT's actual purpose is interaction detection, which is the one thing this repo has no budget for.** If you cannot afford main effects you certainly cannot afford interactions, which are estimated with less precision than the main effects that generated them. Grade (b) by construction.
- **The correct verdict: MVT is not "wrong at this traffic", it is arithmetically unavailable, and /split-test should refuse it rather than cost it out.** This differs from the repo's handling of low-traffic A/B, which it costs out and starts anyway - a defensible asymmetry, because a 2-arm test at 11.6 months can be shortened by pooling and a 128-cell test cannot be shortened at all.

### 6. Sequential / iterative programmes

- **The winner-becomes-control loop has the only peer-reviewed effect size in this dossier.** Mao & Bojinov formalise the value of iterative experimentation (VOIE) as the difference between the second iteration's variant and the first's, and estimate it across seven months of LinkedIn experiments (~200 experiments, per their Figure 3): iterative experimentation delivered **an additional 20% improvement on one of the firm's primary metrics** beyond what single-shot testing would have produced (source 7, grade (a)). This is the formal justification for the repo's "winner becomes the control" rule, and it is stronger evidence than anything supporting any particular way of designing the first challenger.
- **Published win rates, so a programme can be forecast** (all grade (a) or (b), listed with sample):
  - Optimizely, 127,000 experiments: 12% win on the primary metric (source 11).
  - Conversion Team, 2,288 audited tests / 71 engagements, published June 2026: 19.1% stat-sig per test, 25.4% by test group, 50.5% raw winner, 61.1% decisive at test-group level; lead-gen 60.8% raw, the highest of any business model measured (source 9).
  - DRIP, 90+ brands, thousands of tests 2020-2026: 36.3% stat-sig win, 22.1% significant loss, 41.6% inconclusive, 62.1% of decisive outcomes are wins (source 10).
  - VWO ~14%, CXL/Convert ~20%, cited as the industry band of 25-30% by DRIP and 10-25% as "healthy" elsewhere (grade (c) secondhand aggregation).
  - Booking.com, Google Ads, Netflix: ~10% success (source 6 Table 2, citing Manzi 2012 / Thomke 2020 / Moran 2007); Microsoft 33%; Bing 15%; Airbnb Search 8% (source 6, grade (a)).
- **Average lift per winning test is small and the datasets agree.** DRIP: median winner +1.88% CR, +2.77% RPV; mean +2.91% CR, +4.15% RPV; top decile +9.50% RPV (source 10, grade (b)). Georgiev's 85-test GoodUI analysis: mean 3.77%, median 3.92%, with 58% of all tests between -3% and +10% (source 23, grade (b)). This corroborates the repo's existing "~4% average across 115 tests" figure from an independent dataset and should replace any use of WiderFunnel's 49%.
- **Programme velocity and cumulative expectation, with numbers to set expectations against.** DRIP's own brackets: median 14 tests/year, top quartile 24+, top decile 82+; median test duration 42 days (P25 28, P75 51, P90 76); and their stated forecast - "at 14 tests per year with a 36.3% win rate, expect roughly 5 winners producing a cumulative 14% RPV improvement annually" (source 10, grade (b)). **At 30-300 leads/month a page supports roughly 1 to 10 tests a year, not 14**, so the honest annual expectation is one to three winners and a cumulative improvement in the low tens of percent - which matches the repo's existing "10 to 25% improvement, not a doubling".
- **How many tests before diminishing returns?** No source gives a number. NN/g gives the qualitative marker - "years of gradual fixes yield minimal improvements" is one of its five triggers for a rebuild (source 20, grade (c)) - and this repo's existing rule ("three iterations of the same idea read flat = kill") is the operational equivalent at the axis level. The defensible statement is that diminishing returns are detected per axis, not per programme: an axis that reads flat three times is exhausted, and the programme continues on the remaining axes.
- **What the second challenger should flip.** No direct evidence was found for "flip a different axis next" specifically. The supporting argument is indirect but consistent: Mao & Bojinov establish that iteration itself carries value (source 7, grade (a)); Wesseling argues 10 A/B tests across several locations gather more behavioural insight than one big multi-arm test in one place (source 1, grade (c)); and a within-arm re-test of the same axis is the "three iterations flat" case the repo already kills. Flipping a fresh axis also keeps each test's effect size in the resolvable range, since an already-won axis has had its remaining headroom removed. Grade (c), reasoned.

### 7. Specific to low traffic

- **Ship-and-watch-the-trend is a named, published method, and it is what CXL tells low-traffic sites to do.** "If you don't have enough traffic to test, just roll out the changes (all at once) and observe the impact on your KPIs. Don't be timid and just change 1 word somewhere. Implement all your hypotheses at once, or go for a radical change. Avoid doing meek changes and hoping for a 5% lift, go for a 30%, 50% or 150% lift - positive change big enough that you will notice right away on your bank account or in the number of incoming leads." Its stated caveats: be aware of seasonality and changes in traffic sources, and segment in analytics as much as possible (source 3, grade (c)).
- **Pre/post has a formal version and the repo should use its vocabulary if it uses the method at all.** Interrupted time series: there is no fixed minimum number of data points, because power depends on the distribution of points either side of the change, within-data variability, effect strength and seasonality; ITS controls for pre-existing trends and seasonality in a way naive pre/post does not; and **controlled ITS adds a control series to absorb events concurrent with the change** (source 33, grade (a)). For this repo the available control series is the obvious one and it is already in hand: other pages, other clients in the same trade, or the same page's non-paid traffic. A pre/post with no control series is the weakest read available and should be labelled as such.
- **Sequential (run A for a period, then B for an equal period) is documented with hard preconditions.** It doubles the traffic each variant sees and is faster than a proper split, but "it's not scientific, it's not really apples to apples", and it requires choosing a period that historically performs evenly and running identical calendar windows (both Monday-Sunday). It "will not work (at all)" with significant changes in traffic sources, ad campaigns, holiday season, or a PR spike (source 3, grade (c)). Given that this repo's pages are paid-traffic landing pages for local services, **the precondition "no changes in traffic sources or ad campaigns" is routinely violated**, which makes sequential the wrong fallback here specifically - and is a point in favour of the repo's existing cookie-based split.
- **Pooling across clients is this repo's best available move and the literature both supports and bounds it.** Support: hierarchical/Bayesian models let studies borrow strength via exchangeability, gaining precision at the cost of some bias, with a hyperparameter controlling borrowing strength - small values pool aggressively, large values approach independent analysis (source from the hierarchical-borrowing literature, grade (a)); "a prior built from real past experiments sharpens the next posterior and lowers the sample size needed" (grade (a)). Bound: Longden's objection is precisely that "one website is radically and utterly different from another website... you just cannot control for it", and meta-analysis is degraded by heterogeneity and publication bias (source 23, grade (c) for the objection, (a) for the heterogeneity mechanism). **The practical resolution is the repo's own: pool one identical structural variant within one trade, and report per-host.** Pooling identical structures within a trade is the narrowest exchangeability assumption available, and it is the assumption a hierarchical model would want.
- **Bayesian with informative priors is the right default read at this traffic, and the priors should come from somewhere specific.** A weakly-informative JZS Cauchy prior scaled to the MDE requires no history; for conversion rates a Beta prior centred on past experiments works, and empirical Bayes is preferred wherever history exists; **flat priors throw away information you have** (source 29, grade (c) vendor, method standard). Tight priors shrink estimates and prevent overreaction to fleeting spikes - which is exactly the winner's-curse failure mode at 3% power. Type-I robustness to prior choice is available via e-values, because the martingale property holds for any proper prior (source 30, grade (a)). The honest caveat: narrow intervals or high posterior thresholds still need larger samples, so Bayesian does not manufacture information (source 29). And per CXL/Gershoff, the Bayesian escape from the multiple-comparisons problem "assumes you have a strong prior that the variations are indeed the same", i.e. it is partial pooling, not a free lunch (source 1, grade (c)).
- **Painted-door tests answer a different question than a landing-page split, and their validity limits matter here.** They measure intent by showing an entry point to something that does not exist and counting attempts (source 31, grade (c)). The objections: you cannot verify clicks would have become purchases, some are accidental or curious; the method "lacks the all-important why"; the data "may not reflect true consumer interest or market demand"; and there is churn risk from a door that opens onto nothing (sources 31, 32, grade (c)). For a local-service page the only clean application is **offer-level demand** (would anyone ask for a same-day slot, a fixed-price package, a finance option) *before* building a challenger around that offer - and it must never be used on a page where a real lead is expected, because the cost of disappointing a plumbing prospect is a lost job, not a lost click.
- **The traffic-independent alternatives that should run alongside, not instead of, the split.** User testing at 5-10 people, or 20-50 for a comparison read (sources 3, 4, 24); message testing on copy clarity / relevance / compulsion / differentiation (source 3); calling existing customers, which works at 10 or 45 customers and should ask about their buying process rather than satisfaction scores (source 3); cross-browser and cross-device walkthroughs, described as low-hanging fruit that can bring considerable conversion improvements (source 3); and page speed. Grade (c) throughout, but all are cheap and none consume test traffic.
- **The single most useful low-traffic reframe found: measure closer to the page.** Both Optimizely and VWO advise tracking micro-conversions - "avoid measuring final conversions a few pages ahead" (source 12), and VWO adds the necessary discipline of knowing the micro-to-macro conversion ratio so the macro effect can be inferred (source 16). Grade (c), unanimous. For this repo the micro-conversion nearer the page than "Lead" is form-start, phone-tap and scroll-to-form - which raises the event count by a large multiple at the cost of measuring intent rather than money. **Correct use: as a secondary metric that diagnoses a flat primary, never as the ship criterion.** The repo's primary must stay leads, per its existing rule.

## Claims that did not survive

- **"WiderFunnel's LIFT model produces a 49.0% average lift on lead-gen sites."** Carried by third parties as a WiderFunnel self-reported aggregate with no sample size, date, methodology or handling of selection bias, and absent from Conversion.com's own framework page (sources 25, 26). It contradicts every measured dataset here: median winner +1.88% (source 10, 90+ brands), mean 3.77% / median 3.92% (source 23, 85 tests), and the repo's own ~4% across 115 tests. Do not use it as an expected lift; use LIFT as a page-reading checklist only.
- **"More variants is strictly better because ideas are fungible."** The argument is coherent and internally consistent (source 2), but its author states the precondition at 500 conversions/month minimum, 1,000 preferred, and declines to test sites below ~300/month at all. Quoting the methodology without its floor inverts its conclusion at 30-300 leads/month.
- **"Bayesian methods solve the multiple-comparisons problem, so extra arms are free."** Eppo states Bayesian analysis is exempt from the correction (source 14) and Dynamic Yield's chief data scientist says a Bayesian approach remedies it (source 1). But Gershoff's qualification in the same article is decisive: this assumes a strong prior that the variations are in fact the same, which is partial pooling - it buys variance reduction by shrinking toward a common mean, at the cost of bias toward "no difference" (source 1, grade (c)). It does not create the leads an extra arm needs, and sample pollution is untouched by it.
- **"Session recordings and heatmaps are the highest-yield hypothesis inputs."** Asserted by the framework that weights them (source 5) and the agency that attributes its 36.3% win rate to them (source 10), but no study ranks research inputs by subsequent win rate, and DRIP's own page states no internal data-backed vs not comparison was made. Worse, CXL's low-traffic guidance demotes real-user heatmaps specifically because they need statistically valid sample sizes a low-traffic site does not have (source 3). Session replays survive as a strong input on mechanism grounds; heatmaps do not survive at this traffic.
- **"Generate several candidate variants and have a human or model pick the best one."** Selection ability is the weak link, not generation. On 330 real A/B tests, an LLM judge on screenshots reached κ = 0.141 unconditional and was inconclusive on the trustworthy label subset; a 2.8x more expensive model was statistically equivalent; and prompt redesign, stimulus fidelity and change-type priors all failed their pre-registered gates (source 8, grade (a)). Only confident-agreement gating showed signal (κ = 0.311), flagged exploratory by its own authors. Generating candidates to widen the search is defensible; claiming the pick is informed is not.
- **"Curated CRO case libraries are usable evidence."** 44% of ground-truth labels in a leading agency's case library come from non-significant tests, and a model agreed more with the unreliable labels than the reliable ones (source 8, grade (a)). Kohavi's teardown is the concrete instance: a widely shared "337% lift" case ran 82 vs 75 users with 3 vs 12 conversions, at roughly 3% pre-experiment power, giving a false-positive risk of about 63% even granting the most generous success rate (source 6). Filter any pattern library to significant tests before treating it as a prior.
- **"Sequential testing is a reasonable low-traffic substitute for a split."** It is documented (source 3) but its stated preconditions - no changes in traffic sources, no ad campaigns, no seasonality, no PR spikes - are violated by definition on a paid-traffic local-service landing page. It is the wrong fallback for this repo specifically.
- **"Lower your significance threshold to run faster at low traffic."** Offered by Optimizely for low-traffic sites (source 12) and by CXL as stopping at 80% confidence on a large treatment effect (source 3), both explicitly unscientific. Source 6's FPR tables show what it costs: at a 10-15% base success rate, relaxing alpha while power is already low puts false-positive risk among declared winners above 40%. The repo's existing answer - keep alpha at 0.05 and buy power by raising the MDE instead - is the better trade and should stay.
- **"MVT is sometimes worth costing out at low traffic."** It is not. Three axes need ~1,880 leads (5.2 years at 30/month); seven axes need ~49,000 (162 years at 300/month). /split-test should refuse rather than quote.

## The recommended method

An ordered procedure for deriving ONE challenger for a local-service landing page at 30-300 leads/month. Each step names the evidence that feeds the decision and the tier that evidence sits in.

1. **Fix the primary metric and the MDE before looking at the page.** Primary is leads (form, call or text). Compute leads/arm = 16/lift² at the MDE you can afford, and read the months off the current traffic. This ordering exists because source 6 shows a low-power test manufactures both false winners and inflated effects - the affordable effect size determines how radical the challenger must be, so it must be known before design starts. Evidence: source 6 (a), repo's existing sample_size.py.
2. **Take the top item from context/test-queue.md and identify its evidence tier.** Tier 1 = your own prior significant result on a comparable page, or a pattern replicated in significant tests (sources 21, 22, 8 - grade (b)). Tier 2 = session replays of non-converting sessions, form-field drop-off, or sales-call objections (source 3's worked form example, grade (c) but direct observation). Tier 3 = user testing at 5-10 people, exit survey (source 24, grade (c)). Tier 4 = heuristic analysis of the page's own structure (source 3, grade (c), explicitly "not guaranteed to be optimal"). **Write the tier into the test log.** A tier-4 hypothesis is legitimate for a first test on a never-tested page - that is what heuristic analysis is for - but it must not be recorded as though it were tier 1.
3. **Read the control's shape and check for a stronger constraint than structure.** Run the audit. If the queue item's evidence points at a specific failure that the shape does not capture - a form field everyone abandons, an objection nobody answers - that failure is the hypothesis and it outranks a mechanical axis flip. Evidence: source 3's references-field case (grade (c)); source 9's finding that copy and social proof win more often than layout (grade (b)), which says the content of the change matters more than the geometry when you have evidence about the content.
4. **Derive the challenger by flipping the axes the audit measured, exactly as invert.py does, and hold the substance fixed.** The axes are the LIFT factors made measurable: length and CTA pattern are Distraction, form depth and field count are Friction/Anxiety, proof placement is Anxiety, hero shape and section order are Clarity and Relevance (source 25, grade (c)). Offer, guarantee, claims, numbers, design system, form handler, thank-you page and tracking never move. Evidence for holding substance fixed: the attribution problem is already maximal across seven axes (source 4, grade (c)); moving the offer as well makes the result uninterpretable even as a shape claim.
5. **Gate the challenger on noticeability before building it, using PXL's operational tests.** Would a person shown control then challenger for **5 seconds** be able to tell them apart? Does the challenger **add or remove** something, not merely rearrange it? Is the difference **above the fold**? If any answer is no, the challenger is too close to the control and the test will read flat at any affordable sample. Evidence: source 5 (grade (c)), and the effect-size arithmetic in source 6 (grade (a)) that makes a subtle difference unresolvable here.
6. **Sanity-check the size of the bet against the MDE.** Is the flip plausibly worth the lift the test is powered to detect - the "apple or orange, not red apple vs green apple" test? If not, flip more axes. If the challenger is so different that it changes what is being offered, flip fewer. Evidence: sources 16, 12 (grade (c), unanimous vendor); source 6 (a).
7. **Generate candidates if it is cheap, but pick by rule, not by taste.** Widening the search is defensible - ResearchXL's own step is to hypothesise as many solutions as possible (source 24, grade (c)). Selecting among them by judgement is not - κ = 0.141 for a model, and a third-win/third-flat/third-lose distribution for humans (sources 8, 6, grade (a)). So: generate 2-3 candidate briefs from the same axis flips, then select the one that (i) scores highest on step 5's noticeability gate, (ii) rests on the highest evidence tier from step 2, and (iii) is cheapest to build - all three being observable properties rather than predictions of outcome. If independent passes disagree about which is best, that disagreement is information: prefer the one whose hypothesis is most directly traceable to step 2's evidence, since no tie-break based on predicted performance is supportable. Evidence: source 8's abstention result (grade (a), exploratory).
8. **Optionally validate the pick without spending traffic.** Recruit 20-50 people, split them so half see only the control first and half only the challenger first (order anchors opinion), then show both side by side, unmarked then marked, and collect **written** answers. A 70/30 split either way is a signal; anything between 30/70 and 50/50 means the change is not noticed and you are back to step 5. Evidence: source 4 (grade (c), author's n>1,000 recordings). This buys a sanity check, not a prediction - source 8's result stands.
9. **Build it, launch it, and do not touch it.** Repo's existing rules: whole weeks, two minimum, no peeking, no mid-flight edits.
10. **Read it with an informative prior, not a flat one.** Centre a Beta prior on the baseline conversion rate this trade actually shows across your portfolio, and let it shrink the estimate. This is the formal version of the repo's 13% winner's-curse haircut and it is better because it scales with realised power rather than being constant. Evidence: source 29 (grade (c) vendor, standard method); source 30 (a) for Type-I robustness; source 6's Gelman-Carlin exaggeration ratio (a) for why shrinkage is required at low power.
11. **Record the result as a claim about a shape, not about an element.** "Short page with form-in-hero and proof-first beat the long offer-first page, +X% leads [interval]" is defensible. "Moving the form up lifts leads X%" is not, because six other axes moved with it. Evidence: source 4 (grade (c)); the attribution limit is intrinsic, not fixable at this traffic.
12. **Winner becomes the control; the next challenger flips a different axis.** This is the step with the best evidence in the whole dossier - iterative experimentation was worth an additional 20% on a primary metric across seven months of LinkedIn experiments (source 7, grade (a)). An axis that has read flat three times is exhausted; move on rather than re-testing it.
13. **Log the failure as carefully as the win.** Booking.com's published practice is a central repository of successes *and* failures, precisely so the knowledge is reusable (source 28, grade (b)). A flat result on a shape is the thing that makes the next hypothesis tier 1 instead of tier 4.

## How many candidates to generate

**Generate 2 to 3 candidate briefs. Build and run exactly 1.**

The maths, stated so it can be checked:

- Leads per arm = 16/lift² at 80% power and two-sided alpha, baseline cancelling (source 6, grade (a)). At a 30% MDE this is 174 leads per arm for a two-arm test.
- Each additional test arm raises family-wise alpha as 1-(1-0.05)^k and forces per-comparison alpha down to 0.05/k under Bonferroni (source 1 for the formula, grade (a)), which raises leads per arm to 211 at k=2 (+21%), 233 at k=3 (+33%), 248 at k=4 (+42%).
- Because the extra arm needs its own sample *and* raises everyone's threshold, total leads needed goes 349 → 634 → 931 → 1,239 for 2, 3, 4, 5 arms. A third arm is not +50%, it is **+82% of total leads**; a fourth is +167%.
- In months at the traffic this repo serves: at 30 leads/month, 11.6 months for two arms, 21.1 for three, 31.0 for four. At 100/month: 3.5, 6.3, 9.3. At 300/month: 1.2, 2.1, 3.1.
- Sample pollution makes the true penalty larger than the alpha penalty, and it grows with duration: a returning visitor with cleared cookies lands in the same arm 50% of the time with one challenger, 25% with three, and polluted arms converge toward identical conversion rates (source 1, grade (c)).
- The threshold, therefore: **a third arm costs about one extra month above 300 leads/month and is defensible there; below 300 it roughly doubles a test that is already 12-21 months long, and is not.** Multivariate is unavailable at any point on this curve - three axes need ~1,880 leads (5.2 years at 30/month), seven need ~49,000 (162 years at 300/month).

Why generate more than one candidate if only one runs: generation is free and widens the search, which is the defensible half of Anderson's beta argument (source 2) and is ResearchXL's own documented step (source 24). Why not more than three: the selection step has near-zero demonstrated accuracy (κ = 0.141, source 8, grade (a)), so additional candidates add build cost and decision latency without improving the expected quality of the pick. Two to three is the point where the selection rule in step 7 - noticeability, evidence tier, build cost, all observable - can still discriminate between them on grounds that do not require predicting the outcome.

## Rules for /split-test

- Two arms. One control, one challenger. Refuse a third arm below 300 leads/month across all arms.
- If leads/month ≥ 300 and a third arm is requested: print total leads needed at Bonferroni-corrected alpha (0.05/k), the months, and the delta against two arms, then require explicit confirmation.
- Never multivariate. Print the cell count, the leads per cell at 0.05/(cells-1), and the years, then refuse. Do not offer it as an option.
- Leads per arm = 16 / lift², times the Bonferroni inflation for k test arms: 1.00 at k=1, 1.21 at k=2, 1.33 at k=3, 1.42 at k=4.
- Generate 2-3 candidate challenger briefs from the axis flips. Build one.
- Select the candidate by observable properties only: noticeability gate, then evidence tier, then build cost. Never by predicted lift.
- Noticeability gate, all three required before build: distinguishable in a 5-second side-by-side; adds or removes something rather than only rearranging; the difference is above the fold.
- Every queue item carries an evidence tier in the log: 1 = own prior significant result or pattern replicated in significant tests; 2 = session replay, form drop-off, or sales-call objection; 3 = user testing or exit survey; 4 = heuristic read of the page's own shape. Tier 4 is allowed on a never-tested page and must be recorded as tier 4.
- A pattern taken from any external case library is tier 1 only if the source test was statistically significant. Otherwise tier 4.
- Substance never moves: offer, guarantee, claims, numbers, design system, form handler, thank-you page, tracking.
- Log the result as a claim about the page shape, never about a single axis. Seven axes moved; no axis gets credit.
- Heatmaps do not qualify as evidence below 300 leads/month - sample too small. Session replays and form analytics do.
- Read the result with a Beta prior centred on the portfolio baseline for that trade, not a flat prior. This replaces the flat 13% winner's-curse haircut.
- Winner becomes the control. The next challenger flips a different axis. An axis flat three times is exhausted and closed.
- Log every flat and every loss with the same detail as a win. A logged flat is what promotes the next hypothesis above tier 4.
- Micro-conversions (form-start, phone-tap, scroll-to-form) are secondary metrics for diagnosing a flat primary. They are never the ship criterion.
- Expected programme, so nobody quits: 10-25% stat-sig win rate per test, median winner around +2 to +4%, 1-3 real winners a year at this traffic. Quote these, never 49%.
